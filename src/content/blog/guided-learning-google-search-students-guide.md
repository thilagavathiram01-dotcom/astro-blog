Google is expanding Guided Learning beyond Gemini and Classroom into Search for student accounts. The feature helps students work through homework problems step by step instead of jumping straight to answers. A pilot starts in select US schools later this month.

This guide explains how Guided Learning in Google Search works, what students and educators should expect, and practical steps to prepare. It draws only from official Google announcements on 3 November 2026.

## What Guided Learning Does in Search

Guided Learning activates when Search detects certain types of homework help requests from student accounts. Instead of a direct answer or AI Overview, the system walks the student through the topic or problem with prompts that encourage critical thinking.

Examples from Google include pasting a complex physics problem or asking for help writing a book report. Students see questions and steps that help them reason through concepts rather than receiving the finished solution.

The feature prioritizes understanding over shortcuts. It builds on the same approach already used in Gemini and now rolling out globally in Google Classroom.

## Current Rollout and School Controls

Guided Learning in Search begins as a pilot in select US schools later in November 2026. Google will collect educator feedback before expanding to more districts in the coming months.

Separately, access to Gemini, Gemini Notebook, Gemini in Classroom, and Google Search will turn off by default for K-12 student accounts. School administrators must explicitly enable each product. The change rolls out in early 2027 after Google shares more details with schools.

This gives districts time to set policies. Educators retain control over when and how students use AI tools.

For the Classroom version that is already globally available, see our existing walkthrough of [Guided Learning in Google Classroom](/blog/guided-learning-google-classroom-guide/).

## How Students Use Guided Learning in Search

Once the pilot reaches a school and an admin enables Search, students can trigger the experience with natural homework queries.

1. Open Google Search on a signed-in student account.
2. Enter a question that looks like homework help, such as a physics problem or a request for guidance on a book report.
3. If the system recognizes the intent, Guided Learning appears with step-by-step prompts.
4. Follow the questions. Answer them in your own words or work through the intermediate steps shown.
5. Continue until you reach a clearer understanding. The tool does not hand over the final answer.

Students stay in the Search interface. No separate app is required. Progress stays private to the student session.

## Tips for Educators and Parents

- Talk to students about the difference between getting an answer and building understanding. Guided Learning is designed for the latter.
- During the pilot, collect feedback on which subjects and problem types work well. Share observations with school administrators.
- Pair Search Guided Learning with the Classroom version so students see consistent coaching across tools.
- Review the upcoming admin controls. Decide in advance which AI products your school will enable for K-12 accounts.
- Encourage students to attempt problems first, then use Guided Learning for stuck points rather than as a first step.

These practices keep the tool aligned with learning goals. Google’s research on Guided Learning in other settings showed higher score gains when students engaged actively rather than receiving direct answers.

## What Comes Next

Google plans to expand the Search pilot based on educator input. Broader availability follows once the experience is refined. The default-off controls for K-12 accounts arrive in early 2027.

Administrators will receive more specifics in the coming months. Districts can use the interim period to train staff and set policies.

## Conclusion

Guided Learning in Google Search gives student accounts a structured way to work through homework without shortcutting the learning process. The pilot starts soon in select US schools, and classroom controls give educators the final say.

Prepare by reviewing the Classroom version, discussing the feature with students, and watching for admin updates. The tool works best when it supports thinking rather than replacing it.
