OpenAI published its National Community Compact on November 5, 2026, two days after midterm elections where voters backed measures limiting data center growth in many places. The document sets baseline commitments for future compute campuses that OpenAI owns and operates.

It arrives as communities raise questions about electricity bills, water use, noise, and local jobs. OpenAI also released a companion Compute Campus Policy Blueprint asking lawmakers to apply similar rules more broadly.

The compact builds on earlier Stargate Community plans announced in places such as Georgia and Ohio. It applies only to sites OpenAI itself owns and operates, not every facility run by partners.

## What the Compact Requires

Every covered campus will receive its own local compact shaped by resident input. OpenAI says it will engage communities early enough for feedback to influence plans, name a local point of contact for the life of the site, and publish an annual progress report.

Core commitments include:

- Paying the full cost of electricity used and the grid infrastructure needed to serve the campus. Financial assurances will be posted before major spending so other customers are protected if a project is delayed, reduced, or canceled.
- Developing a water plan for each site, prioritizing closed-loop or low-water cooling, and publishing site-level water and electricity use.
- Publishing construction plans and independent noise studies before operations begin, then annually.
- Avoiding non-disclosure agreements with public officials except for narrow exceptions covering security information, trade secrets, or proprietary details.
- Publishing expected jobs, working with unions and training providers, and reporting on hiring and apprenticeships each year. Local investments will target priorities residents help select.

In Effingham County, Georgia, residents helped direct a $4.8 million grant for free school meals for all 14,000 students in the county. In Pike County, Ohio, OpenAI and SB Energy committed $6 million toward childcare after families identified it as a priority.

## The Policy Blueprint

The companion blueprint asks state utility commissions to require large electricity customers to cover the costs they add to the grid and to protect other ratepayers if projects fall through. It also suggests making it easier for campuses that can safely reduce demand during peak periods to connect.

At the planned Effingham County site, an agreement with Georgia Power allows the utility to cut power delivered to OpenAI by up to 1,000 megawatts during high-demand periods. The blueprint further calls for public reporting on water, energy, noise, and hiring, plus measurable tax-incentive terms with clawbacks if developers miss commitments.

Congress is asked to speed transmission upgrades and expand clean-electricity tax incentives. Developers should share purchasing plans early so local suppliers can compete, and employers should use hiring forecasts with building-trades unions and community colleges.

## What This Means for Residents

The compact gives communities a documented baseline they can track. Annual reports and a named local contact create points of accountability. Publishing water and electricity figures removes some of the opacity that has surrounded many data center projects.

It does not cover partner-owned campuses. Much of OpenAI’s capacity still comes through arrangements with Oracle and other providers. Residents near those sites will need to negotiate separately.

The midterm results showed that electricity costs and local control are politically salient. OpenAI’s move is an attempt to set a higher voluntary standard while urging regulators to make similar protections mandatory industry-wide.

## Tips for Following the Commitments

1. Check the OpenAI announcement and linked PDF compact for the exact language on energy, water, and NDA rules.
2. Watch for the first local compact published for a new owned-and-operated site. It should reflect community input and include the common baseline.
3. Compare annual progress reports against the original commitments on jobs, water use, and grid costs.
4. If your community is considering a large electricity customer of any kind, note the blueprint’s suggestions for ratepayer protections and public reporting.

These steps help turn a company document into something trackable.

## Conclusion

OpenAI’s National Community Compact and Compute Campus Policy Blueprint mark a concrete response to local concerns about AI infrastructure. The company commits to covering its energy costs, publishing impact data, limiting NDAs, and investing in priorities residents select at campuses it owns and operates. The blueprint asks governments to extend similar rules more broadly. Whether the approach spreads will depend on how regulators and other developers respond, and on whether the annual reports show the commitments being met.
