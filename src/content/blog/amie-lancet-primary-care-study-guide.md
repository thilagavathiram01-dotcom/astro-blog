A chatbot that talks to patients before a clinic visit sounds useful until you ask who watched the chat, and what happened when the doctor walked in. On 8 October 2026, Google and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center published a prospective feasibility study of Articulate Medical Intelligence Explorer (AMIE) in The Lancet. Google called it the company's first paper in the main journal of The Lancet.

The study is not a product launch. AMIE is a research diagnostic chatbot. Patients used it ahead of real urgent primary care visits, with physicians supervising every conversation. This guide walks through the official numbers, what they do and do not prove, and how to read the paper without turning a feasibility result into a clinic policy.

If you follow Google's other research tools, the same caution applies as with the [AlphaGenome Atlas variant guide](/blog/alphagenome-atlas-avi-variant-guide/): a public result is a starting point, not a finished clinical workflow.

## What the study actually tested

The Lancet abstract describes a prospective, single-centre, single-arm feasibility study. English-speaking adults interacted with AMIE up to five days before a single-complaint urgent primary care appointment at BIDMC's ambulatory clinic. Physician safety supervisors monitored every interaction and were trained to stop a chat against predefined safety criteria. Transcripts and summaries went to the patient's primary care physician before the visit.

Primary outcomes were the number of supervised conversation safety stops, conversation quality scored by clinical evaluators, and patient and physician experience surveys. The trial is registered as NCT06911398. Alphabet funded the work.

From April to November 2025, 114 patients enrolled. Ninety-eight completed both the AMIE chat and the physician appointment. That completion gap matters. Results describe people who finished the protocol, not everyone who was invited.

## Read the safety and prep numbers first

Zero conversation safety stops were required under the prespecified criteria. Safety supervisors still noted one hallucination and added clinical information in five interactions. A zero-stop result is not the same as a zero-error result. The supervisors were the control, and they did intervene with extra clinical detail in a small number of chats.

Clinical evaluators rated AMIE's conversations favourably in 87 to 100 percent of cases across 17 criteria. Patients rated them favourably in 48 to 96 percent of cases across 16 criteria. The spread on the patient side is the more useful figure. Some quality measures landed near half; others landed near the top. Quote the range, not a single "patients loved it" line.

Physician follow-up was thinner than enrolment. Primary care physicians completed post-surveys in 60 of 98 cases. In 44 of those, the physician had reviewed the AMIE transcript before the visit. They found AMIE helpful for visit preparation in 33 of those 44 cases, which is the 75 percent figure in Google's 8 October summary. They said it might have changed their behaviour in 25 of 44 cases, which matches Google's "more than half" wording.

Google also reported that AMIE's differential diagnoses matched the doctors' final diagnoses 90 percent of the time. Treat that as Google's summary of the paper, and check the full text before you repeat a top-1 versus top-list claim the abstract summary does not spell out.

## Walk through the paper in order

Use this sequence when you brief a team or write a caption.

1. Open the Google post from 8 October 2026 and the Lancet abstract side by side. Note the author line: Google researchers with BIDMC.
2. Record the design before the percentages: single centre, single arm, English-speaking adults, one complaint, urgent primary care, chat up to five days before the visit.
3. Write the funnel: 114 enrolled, 98 completed both the chat and the appointment.
4. Separate safety stops from supervisor notes. Zero stops. One hallucination noted. Clinical information added in five interactions.
5. Report physician uptake with denominators. Surveys in 60 of 98. Transcript reviewed in 44. Helpful for prep in 33 of 44. Possible behaviour change in 25 of 44.
6. Keep Google's 90 percent diagnosis-match line labelled as the company's summary until you have read the methods for how "match" was defined.
7. End with the interpretation sentence in the abstract: further research is needed; this is initial feasibility, not a finished translation into care.

Patient attitudes toward AI improved after the AMIE chat and stayed higher after the physician visit, according to the abstract. That is an attitude shift, not proof that care got better.

## What this study does not show

There is no control arm. Patients were not randomised to AMIE versus usual intake, so you cannot claim the chatbot shortened visits, cut errors, or improved outcomes. The site is one academic primary care clinic. Results do not transfer automatically to emergency departments, paediatrics, non-English visits, or multi-problem appointments.

AMIE is not offered here as a consumer app you can switch on. Google's post still calls it a research system. Supervisors watched live. That oversight is part of the safety result. A chatbot running alone in a waiting room is a different product.

The abstract also does not say AMIE replaced the physician. Transcripts were shared so the clinician could prepare. The visit still happened. Anyone writing "AI saw the patient instead of the doctor" is rewriting the protocol.

## Tips before you cite or act on it

Cite the abstract for enrolment, safety stops, the hallucination note, evaluator ranges, and the 33-of-44 and 25-of-44 physician results. Cite the Google blog when you use the 90 percent diagnosis-match sentence or the "first Lancet main-journal paper" claim.

Do not drop the supervisor. The honest safety sentence is: no chat was stopped under the preset rules, and supervisors still flagged one hallucination and added clinical detail five times.

Say who was excluded by design. Under 18, non-English speakers, and people who did not finish both steps are outside these findings.

If you cover health AI for a newsroom, label AMIE as research. Do not tell readers to skip intake or to paste symptoms into a general chatbot because this paper exists. This article is not medical advice.

Pair the paper with the trial record, NCT06911398, if a reader wants the registered outcomes. Feasibility papers move. The registry is the check on what the team said they would measure.

## Where the result leaves clinic AI

The useful finding is narrow and still worth keeping. In one supervised primary care setting, 98 patients finished a pre-visit AMIE chat, no conversation met the stop rule, and physicians who read the transcript often said it helped them prepare. Patients' ratings were mixed across criteria. Diagnosis match, as Google summarised it, was high, and the authors still call for larger trials.

That is a feasibility bar, cleared with humans in the loop. It is the right size of claim for a first Lancet paper on this system, and the wrong size of claim for an unsupervised diagnostic product.
